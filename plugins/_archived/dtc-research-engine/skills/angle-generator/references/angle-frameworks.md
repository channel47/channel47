# Angle Frameworks — Deep Dive

## Pain-Agitation Angles

The most reliable DTC angle category. Works because humans are more motivated to avoid pain than to gain pleasure.

**How to find them in research:**
- Look for the most emotionally charged language in customer complaints
- Pay attention to cascading consequences: "My dog barks → neighbors complain → I feel anxious → I dread coming home"
- The deeper the cascade, the stronger the angle

**Sub-types:**
- **The Visible Pain**: Problems others can see (embarrassment, social situations)
- **The Hidden Pain**: Problems only the customer knows about (private struggle, internal anxiety)
- **The Compounding Pain**: Problems that get worse over time if unsolved
- **The Transferred Pain**: Problems that affect someone the customer cares about (partner, child, pet)

**Execution tips:**
- Agitate without being cruel — the goal is empathy, not manipulation
- The customer should feel "finally, someone understands" not "this ad is trying to make me feel bad"
- Always resolve the agitation with hope — never leave the customer in pain without a path forward

## Failed-Solution Angles

Extremely effective for products competing in crowded markets. Taps into the customer's learned helplessness and positions the product as the breakthrough.

**How to find them in research:**
- "I've tried X, Y, and Z and nothing works" — this exact pattern appears constantly
- The specific solutions they've tried and WHY they failed
- The emotional toll of repeated failure

**Sub-types:**
- **The Exhausted Buyer**: Emphasize how many things they've tried
- **The Money Pit**: Emphasize how much they've wasted on things that don't work
- **The Time Thief**: Emphasize how long they've been dealing with this
- **The Almost-Solution**: "X was close but fell short because..." — position your product as the final piece

**Execution tips:**
- Name the specific failed solutions — vague "other products" doesn't resonate
- Briefly explain WHY each solution failed, then explain why this one is different
- This angle works best when paired with a mechanism/discovery explanation

## Trigger-Event Angles

Targets the specific moment someone transitions from passive awareness to active searching. These are time-sensitive and emotionally charged.

**How to find them in research:**
- "I finally decided to buy when..."
- "The last straw was..."
- "What made me look into this was..."
- Life changes: new baby, new home, health scare, relationship change

**Sub-types:**
- **The Breaking Point**: When the problem becomes unbearable
- **The Social Trigger**: When someone else notices or comments on the problem
- **The Life Stage Trigger**: When a life change makes the problem relevant
- **The Deadline Trigger**: When there's a time pressure (event, season, visit from in-laws)

**Execution tips:**
- Start the ad at the trigger moment — "The day my neighbor left that note on my door..."
- Create instant recognition — the viewer should think "that happened to me"
- Urgency is built-in with trigger angles — they already feel the need to act NOW

## Identity Angles

Goes deeper than pain — targets how the problem makes the customer see themselves. Powerful because identity change is the most durable motivator.

**How to find them in research:**
- "I feel like a bad [parent/pet owner/person]"
- "People must think I'm [lazy/gross/incompetent]"
- "I used to be someone who..."
- "I want to be the kind of person who..."

**Sub-types:**
- **The Negative Identity**: "Stop being the person who..."
- **The Aspirational Identity**: "Become the person who..."
- **The Social Identity**: "What do others see when they look at you?"
- **The Self-Perception Shift**: "You deserve to feel..."

**Execution tips:**
- Handle with care — identity angles are emotionally potent
- The product should be positioned as enabling the identity shift, not defining them by the problem
- Works best with testimonials that describe transformation in identity terms

## Social Proof Angles

Leverages the human tendency to follow what others do. Especially effective for overcoming skepticism in categories with low trust.

**How to find them in research:**
- Volume indicators: "100,000+ customers", "millions sold"
- Surprising users: "even my veterinarian uses this"
- Transformation stories: dramatic before/after narratives
- Expert endorsements: professional recommendations

**Sub-types:**
- **The Bandwagon**: "Everyone's switching to..."
- **The Authority**: "[Expert type] recommends..."
- **The Transformation**: Before and after stories
- **The Unexpected User**: "[Surprising group] discovered..."

## Discovery / Secret Angles

Positions the product as a hidden solution the market doesn't know about yet. Taps into curiosity and the desire to be "in the know."

**How to find them in research:**
- Unique mechanisms or ingredients that competitors don't use
- "I can't believe I didn't know about this sooner"
- Novel approaches to common problems
- Scientific or technical explanations for why something works

**Sub-types:**
- **The Hidden Method**: "The [industry] doesn't want you to know..."
- **The Accidental Discovery**: "A [scientist/inventor] stumbled on..."
- **The Ancient Wisdom**: "For centuries, [culture] has used..."
- **The Simple Fix**: "It turns out the solution was simpler than anyone thought"

**Execution tips:**
- The "secret" needs to be genuinely interesting, not manufactured
- Explain enough of the mechanism to be credible without being boring
- Works particularly well in YouTube and long-form formats where you have time to build intrigue

## Comparison Angles

Direct or indirect comparison to alternatives. Works when the product has a clear, demonstrable advantage.

**How to find them in research:**
- "X is better than Y because..."
- "I switched from [competitor] to [product] because..."
- Price comparisons customers make themselves
- Feature comparisons in review threads

**Sub-types:**
- **The Direct Comparison**: Product vs. specific competitor
- **The Category Comparison**: Product vs. the old way of doing things
- **The Cost Comparison**: Product cost vs. cost of alternatives / cost of not solving
- **The Time Comparison**: Speed/ease vs. alternatives

## Specificity Angles

Uses concrete, specific data points to build instant credibility. The brain registers specific numbers as more trustworthy than vague claims.

**How to find them in research:**
- Specific statistics from customer surveys or review analysis
- Precise timeframes ("works in 30 seconds", "lasts 6 months")
- Exact percentages or quantities
- Specific use cases with measurable outcomes

**Sub-types:**
- **The Stat**: Lead with a surprising statistic
- **The Timeframe**: Specific "how fast it works" claim
- **The Count**: Number of customers, units sold, reviews
- **The Comparison Number**: "X times more effective than..."

**Execution tips:**
- Odd numbers feel more real than round numbers ("147,000 customers" > "150,000 customers")
- Source the stat if possible — even "based on customer reviews" adds credibility
- Combine specificity with another angle type for maximum impact
