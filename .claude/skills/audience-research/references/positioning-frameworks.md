## April Dunford's Positioning Model

From *Obviously Awesome*. Positioning isn't a tagline exercise — it's a strategic decision about context. The same product positioned differently attracts different customers, competes against different alternatives, and commands different prices.

### The Five Components

Work through these in order. Each one constrains the next.

| Component | Question | Why This Order |
|-----------|----------|----------------|
| **Competitive Alternatives** | What would customers use if you didn't exist? | Grounds everything in reality — not what you wish you competed against, but what you actually displace |
| **Unique Attributes** | What do you have that alternatives lack? | Only matters relative to the alternatives you identified |
| **Value** | What benefit do those attributes enable? | Attributes are features. Value is what the customer gets. |
| **Target Audience** | Who cares most about that value? | The people for whom your unique value is most critical |
| **Market Category** | What frame makes your value obvious? | The context that makes customers "get it" instantly |

### How to Apply This

When analyzing a subject's positioning:

1. Start by listing what their customers would do instead. Not what the founder considers competitors — what the *customer* would use. Often this is "nothing," "spreadsheets," or "hire someone."

2. Identify what the subject does that these alternatives genuinely cannot. Not "better UX" (subjective) but "real-time collaboration on the same document" (objective and verifiable).

3. Translate attributes into outcomes. "Real-time collaboration" → "no more emailing files back and forth, no version conflicts, decisions happen in hours instead of days."

4. Ask: who is *desperate* for that outcome? Not "who might like it" — who would switch today because this value solves a burning problem?

5. What category frame makes this value self-evident? Sometimes the existing category works ("project management software"). Sometimes you need a new frame ("connected workspace" vs. "note-taking app").

### Common Mistakes

- **Starting with the category instead of the alternatives.** "We're an AI-powered productivity tool" is a category statement, not positioning. Start with what you replace.
- **Listing features as unique attributes.** If a competitor also has it, it's not unique. Be ruthless.
- **Targeting too broadly.** "Teams" is not a target audience. "Marketing teams at B2B SaaS companies with 50-200 employees" is.

## Eugene Schwartz's Awareness Levels

From *Breakthrough Advertising*. The single most useful framework for understanding why messaging fails. Most companies write for the wrong awareness level.

### The Five Levels

| Level | What They Know | What They Need to Hear | Competitive Density |
|-------|---------------|----------------------|---------------------|
| **Most Aware** | Know your product, just need a reason to act now | Deals, urgency, social proof, new features | Extremely crowded |
| **Product Aware** | Know solutions exist, comparing options | Why you're better than alternatives, proof, differentiation | Very crowded |
| **Solution Aware** | Know solutions exist in general, haven't chosen a type | Education on approach, why this *kind* of solution works | Moderate |
| **Problem Aware** | Know they have a problem, don't know solutions exist | Agitation of the problem, introduction of the solution category | Light |
| **Unaware** | Don't know they have a problem | Story, analogy, disruption of current thinking | Nearly empty |

### The Strategic Insight

Almost every company targets Most Aware and Product Aware — the bottom of the funnel. These people are comparison-shopping and cost the most to acquire (high competition, high CPCs, lots of noise).

The real opportunity is usually at **Problem Aware** and **Solution Aware**. These audiences are:
- Larger (more people have problems than know about specific products)
- Cheaper to reach (less competition for their attention)
- More loyal once converted (you educated them, so you're the default)
- Harder to serve (requires content, not just ads)

When analyzing a competitive landscape, map where each competitor's messaging lands on this ladder. The level where nobody is speaking is usually the biggest opportunity.

### How to Apply This

For each competitor, classify their primary messaging:

- **Homepage tagline** → What level does it speak to?
- **Blog/content** → What level does it educate at?
- **Ads (if visible)** → What awareness level do they target?
- **Social media** → Where in the awareness spectrum do they engage?

Build a density map:

```
Most Aware:      ████████████████ (crowded — everyone targets here)
Product Aware:   ██████████████   (crowded — comparison content everywhere)
Solution Aware:  ████████         (moderate — some educational content)
Problem Aware:   ███              (light — mostly blog posts nobody reads)
Unaware:         █                (empty — almost nobody operates here)
```

The gap in this map is a gap in the market.

### Matching Messaging to Level

Once you've identified the underserved awareness level, the messaging must match:

| Level | Messaging Approach | Example (for a CRM) |
|-------|-------------------|---------------------|
| **Most Aware** | Offer, urgency, deal | "Upgrade to Pro — 30% off this week" |
| **Product Aware** | Comparison, proof, differentiation | "Why teams switch from Salesforce" |
| **Solution Aware** | Category education, approach validation | "Why a CRM beats your spreadsheet" |
| **Problem Aware** | Problem agitation, outcome promise | "Losing track of customer conversations costs you $47K/year" |
| **Unaware** | Story, pattern interrupt, identity | "The invisible mistake that kills agencies before year 3" |

## Category Design

Based on the *Play Bigger* methodology and Al Ries's *Positioning*. Sometimes the best position isn't inside an existing category — it's creating or reframing the category itself.

### When to Use Category Design

Category creation is warranted when:
- The existing category forces unfavorable comparisons (you'll always be "the worse version of X")
- Your unique value doesn't fit the existing category's evaluation criteria
- The market is mature and all positions within the category are claimed
- You combine elements from multiple categories in a genuinely new way

Category creation is NOT warranted when:
- You're just trying to avoid competition (they'll follow you)
- The "new category" is really just a feature or adjective + existing category
- The market doesn't have the problem you're creating a category around

### The Frame Test

The most powerful positioning move is choosing the right competitive frame. The same product looks different in different frames:

| Product | Frame A | Frame B | Implication |
|---------|---------|---------|-------------|
| Notion | Note-taking app | Connected workspace | A → competes with Evernote (losing). B → creates new category (winning). |
| Basecamp | Project management tool | Calm company toolkit | A → compared to Asana, Monday (feature war). B → compared to chaos (easy win). |
| HEY | Email client | Email rethought | A → compared to Gmail (impossible). B → appeals to people frustrated with email as a category. |

Ask: **"In which frame does the subject's unique value matter most?"**

If they're currently in a frame where their strengths are irrelevant, the biggest positioning win might be reframing, not better messaging within the current frame.

## The Jobs-to-Be-Done Lens

From Clayton Christensen. People don't buy products — they hire them to do a job. The job is the unit of analysis, not the product or the feature.

### How to Identify the Job

The job formula: **"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]."**

Examples:
- "When I'm juggling 15 client projects, I want to see everything in one view, so I can stop things from falling through the cracks."
- "When I'm writing a blog post, I want to know what questions my audience has, so I can write something they'll actually read."

### Why This Matters for Positioning

Competitors who target the same *product category* might serve different *jobs*. Two CRMs might both be "CRM software" but one serves the job "never lose track of a deal" and the other serves "understand my customer relationships deeply."

When you find competitors clustered around one job while a related job goes unserved — that's a positioning opportunity.

### Extracting Jobs from Evidence

| Source | How to Find Jobs |
|--------|-----------------|
| **5-star reviews** | What outcome made them ecstatic? That's the job being perfectly served. |
| **3-star reviews** | What did they wish it also did? That's an adjacent job being poorly served. |
| **1-star reviews** | What job did they hire it for that it failed at? That's a job someone else could own. |
| **Reddit/forum posts** | "I wish there was a tool that..." is a literal job statement. |
| **Search queries** | "How to [outcome]" queries reveal jobs being self-served. |

## Competitive Frame Selection

This is the meta-skill that ties the other frameworks together. Choosing the right competitive frame is the single highest-leverage positioning decision.

### The Three Frame Options

| Frame Type | Definition | When to Use | Risk |
|------------|-----------|-------------|------|
| **Head-to-head** | Compete in the existing category, claim superiority | When you genuinely win on the category's established criteria | Feature war, price war, incumbents have advantage |
| **Subcategory** | Create a segment within the existing category | When you serve a specific audience or use case better than generalists | Market might be too small, hard to be discovered |
| **New category** | Redefine the problem and create a new competitive frame | When your value doesn't map to existing categories | Expensive to educate market, might not catch on |

### Decision Framework

```
Can you win the existing category on its own terms?
├── Yes → Head-to-head. Own the #1 position.
├── Maybe → Subcategory. Own a specific niche within it.
└── No → Is your value genuinely different, not just worse?
    ├── Yes → New category. Reframe the problem.
    └── No → Improve the product before positioning it.
```

## Synthesizing Frameworks

These frameworks shouldn't produce separate sections in your analysis. They should produce *one integrated perspective*. Here's how they combine:

1. **Start with Dunford** — What does this replace? What's uniquely valuable? For whom?
2. **Apply Schwartz** — At what awareness level is the market underserved?
3. **Use JTBD** — What job is being hired for, and what jobs are unserved?
4. **Test the frame** — Is the current category the right one? Would a reframe unlock value?
5. **Synthesize** — The position that emerges should be one that exploits the gap (Dunford), speaks at the right awareness level (Schwartz), serves an underserved job (JTBD), and sits in the right competitive frame (category design).

When frameworks conflict — and they sometimes will — the tiebreaker is always customer evidence. What do real people say, search for, complain about, and pay for? That trumps any model.
